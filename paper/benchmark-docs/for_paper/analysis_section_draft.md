# 5. Analysis and Discussion

## 5.1 Interpreting the Performance Hierarchy

The three-tier structure that emerged from our benchmark---with a clear separation at the 82% and 70% accuracy thresholds---provides an empirically grounded framework for assessing LLM readiness for financial viability analysis. The 12.75 percentage-point gap between proprietary models (78.80%) and open-source models (66.05%) aligns with, but exceeds, the 8--10 pp gaps reported in prior general-purpose reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). This amplified gap in our domain-specific evaluation suggests that financial reasoning places disproportionate demands on capabilities that currently differentiate proprietary from open-source architectures, including extended numerical precision, multi-step compositional reasoning, and contextual application of domain-specific formulas.

Claude Opus 4.6's leading position at 88.47% accuracy with 7.83% MAPE represents a notable achievement, yet it also underscores the limitations of current systems. A 7.83% MAPE implies that even the best model produces estimates that deviate from oracle values by approximately one-thirteenth of the true value on average. In high-stakes financial decision-making, where investment thresholds and go/no-go decisions may hinge on single-digit percentage margins, this level of error remains consequential. The result positions LLMs as useful screening and approximation tools rather than replacements for rigorous financial modeling.

The performance spread across the nine models is itself informative. From Claude Opus 4.6 at 88.47% to Gemini 3 Flash at 57.64%, the 30.83 pp range demonstrates that "LLM performance on financial tasks" is not a monolithic quantity---model selection matters profoundly. Similarly, the MAPE spread from 7.83% (Opus) to 41.57% (Gemini Flash) represents a fivefold difference in expected error magnitude, underscoring that model choice has direct consequences for the reliability of financial outputs.

## 5.2 The Computational Depth Hypothesis

Our per-metric analysis reveals a consistent pattern: model performance degrades as the number of intermediate computational steps increases. Simple ratio metrics (gross margin, operating margin) require one or two arithmetic operations and yield high accuracy across all tiers. NPV and IRR calculations, by contrast, require iterative discounting across multiple periods, aggregation of heterogeneous cash flows, and in the case of IRR, implicit root-finding---a chain of 10 or more dependent operations for a typical 5-year projection.

We propose a "computational depth" hypothesis: LLM accuracy in financial calculations degrades approximately linearly with the logarithm of the number of dependent computational steps. This hypothesis is supported by the observed difficulty-level trajectories (Section 4.4), where each incremental level of complexity---which roughly doubles the number of required operations---produces a consistent 11--15 pp accuracy decline for Tier 1 models and a steeper 13--18 pp decline for lower tiers. This finding echoes observations from Dziri et al. (2023), who demonstrated systematic compositionality failures in LLMs on multi-step reasoning tasks, and extends them into the quantitative financial domain.

The bimodal distribution observed for break-even analysis (Section 4.3) provides additional evidence for a threshold effect in computational reasoning. Break-even calculation requires the model to correctly identify fixed costs, variable costs per unit, and revenue per unit, then apply the correct algebraic formula. Models that possess a reliable internal representation of this formula perform consistently well; those that do not fail dramatically. This threshold behavior contrasts with the more gradual degradation observed for NPV, suggesting that different financial metrics impose qualitatively different cognitive demands on LLMs.

## 5.3 Error Taxonomy and Implications

The distribution of error types across model tiers reveals important architectural insights. The preponderance of arithmetic errors in Tier 3 models (41.7% of errors) suggests fundamental limitations in numerical processing. These models appear to struggle not merely with financial reasoning but with basic quantitative manipulation---a finding consistent with Lightman et al. (2023), who showed that smaller language models exhibit systematic arithmetic failures that scale with operand magnitude.

The prominence of formula selection errors in Tier 2 models (27.3% of errors) points to a different failure mode. These models possess sufficient arithmetic capability but sometimes retrieve or construct incorrect financial formulas. For example, a Tier 2 model might compute a payback period using undiscounted cash flows when discounted values are appropriate, or apply a simple interest formula where compound interest is required. This error type suggests that mid-capability models have partial but unreliable financial knowledge, leading to plausible but incorrect methodological choices.

Temporal reasoning errors, concentrated in advanced cases, represent perhaps the most theoretically interesting failure mode. These errors arise when models must track cash flows across periods, correctly apply discount factors to specific time indices, or handle irregular payment schedules. The concentration of these errors in advanced cases (38.6% of errors at that level) implies that temporal financial reasoning requires a level of structured state management that current transformer architectures handle imperfectly, even at large scale.

## 5.4 Scenario Sensitivity and Robustness

The finding that pessimistic scenarios produce the lowest accuracy (ensemble mean 72.1% vs. 79.4% for normal scenarios) has practical implications. In real-world financial analysis, pessimistic projections are arguably the most critical for risk assessment and investment decision-making. The reduced accuracy in precisely this scenario type suggests that LLMs may systematically underperform in the contexts where analytical rigor matters most.

The cross-scenario stability of Claude Opus 4.6 (4.8 pp range) versus the instability of Grok Code (18.3 pp range) indicates that robustness to scenario framing is a distinguishing characteristic of high-performing models. Weaker models appear to be influenced by scenario labels or narrative framing in ways that distort their quantitative outputs, a phenomenon that may be related to the anchoring biases documented in LLM reasoning by Jones and Steinhardt (2022). This finding suggests that scenario-robustness testing should be a standard component of any evaluation framework for financial AI systems.

## 5.5 Comparison with Prior Work

Our benchmark contributes to a growing literature on LLM evaluation in specialized quantitative domains. Compared to the FinBen benchmark (Xie et al., 2024), which evaluates LLMs on financial NLP tasks such as sentiment analysis and named entity recognition, our study focuses on computational financial reasoning---a complementary but distinct capability. While FinBen reports top-model accuracy exceeding 90% on classification tasks, our benchmark yields lower peak accuracy (88.47%), reflecting the additional demands of open-ended numerical computation.

The MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) evaluates mathematical reasoning more broadly and reports similar performance hierarchies among LLMs, with top models achieving 80--90% on competition-level problems. Our results suggest that financial calculation presents comparable difficulty to competition mathematics, with the added challenge of domain-specific formula selection and multi-variable scenario management.

BloombergGPT (Wu et al., 2023) demonstrated that domain-specific pretraining can improve financial NLP performance. Our benchmark did not include domain-specifically trained models, but the strong performance of general-purpose Tier 1 models suggests that scale and general reasoning capability may be sufficient for financial viability calculations, potentially reducing the need for domain-specific fine-tuning for this task class.

Notably, the mid-tier results are also instructive. GPT-5.3 at 78.26% (MAPE 18.73%) and Claude Haiku 4 at 75.13% (MAPE 22.14%) occupy a performance band where outputs are directionally correct but carry sufficient error to be misleading in borderline viability decisions. DeepSeek Chat at 69.71% (MAPE 27.86%) straddles the boundary between Tier 2 and Tier 3, illustrating that the open-source frontier is approaching but has not yet reached the reliability threshold required for unsupervised financial analysis.

## 5.6 Implications for Financial AI Applications

Our findings carry several implications for the deployment of LLMs in financial viability analysis:

First, the Tier 1 models evaluated here achieve sufficient accuracy for preliminary screening and rapid prototyping of financial models. An 88.47% accuracy rate with 7.83% MAPE positions Claude Opus 4.6 as an effective first-pass estimator that can identify obviously viable or non-viable projects, reserving detailed human analysis for borderline cases. Claude Sonnet 4.6 (86.31%, MAPE 10.27%) and GPT-5.4 (83.92%, MAPE 12.41%) offer similar utility at potentially lower cost points.

Second, the systematic nature of error types suggests that hybrid architectures---combining LLM-based reasoning with deterministic calculation modules for well-defined formulas---could substantially reduce error rates. If arithmetic and formula selection errors were eliminated through symbolic computation, the residual error rate for Tier 1 models would drop to approximately 3--5%, approaching the level required for production financial analysis.

Third, the difficulty-dependent performance degradation implies that LLM-assisted financial analysis should incorporate complexity-aware confidence estimation. Models should signal reduced confidence on advanced cases involving multi-period projections, multiple financing sources, or complex tax structures, enabling human analysts to allocate review effort efficiently.

Fourth, the 12.75 pp proprietary-open-source gap has implications for equitable access to financial AI tools. If only proprietary models deliver reliable financial analysis, cost barriers may limit access for small businesses and entrepreneurs in developing economies---precisely the populations that stand to benefit most from democratized financial planning tools.

## 5.7 Limitations

Several limitations qualify our findings. First, our oracle values were computed using deterministic financial formulas applied to synthetic project data. Real-world financial viability analysis involves subjective judgments, market assumptions, and qualitative factors that our benchmark does not capture. Second, our evaluation treats each metric independently, whereas real financial analysis involves interdependent metrics whose joint accuracy may differ from marginal accuracy. Third, our benchmark evaluates single-turn computation; iterative refinement through multi-turn dialogue, which is the intended use case for the AppViabilidadNegocios platform, may yield different performance characteristics. Fourth, model performance is likely to evolve rapidly as new versions are released, and our results represent a snapshot in time rather than a permanent ranking. Finally, the proprietary vs. open-source comparison is confounded by model size, training data, and other factors that we cannot fully control for.
