# 3. Related Work

FinanceBench sits at the intersection of three active research areas: general LLM evaluation, financial AI systems, and numerical reasoning. We survey each area and identify the specific gap our benchmark addresses.

## 3.1 LLM Evaluation Benchmarks

The past three years have seen rapid development of standardized evaluation frameworks for large language models. HELM (Liang et al., 2023) established the paradigm of holistic, multi-metric evaluation by assessing over 30 models across 42 scenarios—measuring not only accuracy but also calibration, robustness, fairness, and efficiency. MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) and its successor MMLU-Pro (Yuan et al., 2024) broadened coverage to 57 knowledge domains, including basic finance, through multiple-choice questions that test factual recall and surface-level reasoning. BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2022) pushed further with over 200 tasks designed to probe capabilities beyond current model limits, including arithmetic and logical reasoning subtasks. Together with GLUE and SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2019), which established the standard of public leaderboards and blind evaluation splits, these benchmarks have created a robust infrastructure for systematic model comparison. More recently, Chatbot Arena (Wang et al., 2024) introduced crowdsourced pairwise evaluation with both human and LLM judges, enabling preference-based ranking across dozens of models.

Despite their breadth, these benchmarks share a critical limitation for our purposes: none evaluates the specific combination of arithmetic precision, formula application, and multi-step cascading computation required for structured financial analysis. MMLU includes finance questions, but a model can correctly identify that NPV is "the sum of discounted cash flows" without being able to compute one. BIG-bench includes arithmetic tasks, but these are orders of magnitude simpler than computing a 5-year income statement with loan amortization schedules and tax loss carryforward. FinanceBench fills this gap by requiring models to *perform* complete financial calculations, not merely *recognize* financial concepts.

## 3.2 Financial AI Systems

Financial NLP has emerged as a distinct subfield with dedicated models, datasets, and benchmarks. BloombergGPT (Wu et al., 2023) demonstrated that domain-specific pretraining on financial corpora yields significant improvements on financial NLP tasks such as named entity recognition, sentiment analysis, and question answering. FinGPT (Yang et al., 2023) pursued the complementary goal of open-source accessibility, providing data pipelines and lightweight fine-tuning recipes for financial applications. Two comprehensive surveys—Li et al. (2023) at ICAIF and Nie et al. (2024)—map the landscape of LLMs in finance, covering zero-shot and fine-tuned approaches across tasks from sentiment classification to algorithmic trading. Both surveys identify structured financial analysis as an underexplored evaluation dimension.

Among benchmarks, FinBen (Xie et al., 2024), published at NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks, represents the most comprehensive financial evaluation to date: over 40 datasets spanning 20+ tasks including information extraction, question answering, summarization, sentiment analysis, stock prediction, and credit scoring, with results for 20+ LLMs. PIXIU (Xie et al., 2023) similarly provides a financial instruction dataset and evaluation benchmark centered on classification and prediction tasks. FinQA (Chen et al., 2021) comes closest to our work by requiring numerical reasoning over financial tables extracted from earnings reports, evaluating whether models can construct correct arithmetic expressions from tabular data. FinanceAgentBench (2025) extends evaluation to agentic workflows, assessing LLMs on multi-step equity research tasks involving information gathering and synthesis.

However, a fundamental gap persists across all existing financial benchmarks: they evaluate language understanding *about* finance—extracting information, classifying sentiment, answering questions from documents—but not the ability to *perform* financial analysis. No existing benchmark asks a model to take raw business inputs (investment costs, revenue projections across scenarios, expense structures, financing terms) and produce a complete, internally consistent financial projection including income statements, cash flows, NPV, IRR, and payback period. FinanceBench is designed as the computational complement to FinBen's linguistic evaluation: where FinBen asks "Can the model understand financial text?", FinanceBench asks "Can the model *do* financial math?"

## 3.3 Numerical Reasoning in LLMs

A growing body of work has documented both the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in numerical and mathematical reasoning. Chain-of-Thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) demonstrated that eliciting step-by-step reasoning dramatically improves performance on arithmetic and logical problems, establishing a baseline technique that has since become standard practice. GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) provided a benchmark of grade-school math word problems with step-by-step solutions, while MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021b) raised the bar to competition-level mathematical problem solving requiring symbolic manipulation and multi-step proofs. DROP (Dua et al., 2019) introduced discrete reasoning over paragraphs—counting, summing, and comparing quantities extracted from natural language—demonstrating that even simple arithmetic becomes challenging when embedded in textual context.

More recently, NumericBench (Li et al., 2024) decomposed numerical ability into six atomic skills (number recognition, arithmetic operations, contextual retrieval, comparison, summarization, and logical reasoning) and showed that even state-of-the-art models like GPT-4o fail systematically on seemingly simple numerical tasks. Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022) demonstrated that domain-specialized training on scientific and mathematical corpora yields substantial improvements on STEM benchmarks, suggesting that analogous specialization may be needed for financial computation. A recent survey on mathematical reasoning in LLMs (Wang et al., 2025) frames the broader challenges, including tool-augmented reasoning and test-time scaling, that are directly relevant to financial analysis.

Financial viability analysis represents a uniquely challenging test of numerical reasoning. Unlike GSM8K problems that require 2–5 arithmetic steps, a single FinanceBench case demands a 12-step computation chain where each step depends on previous results: investment aggregation feeds into financing gap calculation, which determines loan amortization schedules, which produce interest expenses that interact with operating income through tax loss carryforward rules, ultimately cascading into NPV and IRR computations. A small error in early steps—misapplying a growth rate or miscalculating depreciation—propagates through all downstream computations, amplifying into large deviations in final metrics. This cascading error property, combined with domain-specific conventions (French amortization, frequency normalization, multi-scenario analysis), makes financial analysis a stress test that existing mathematical benchmarks do not capture.

## 3.4 Domain-Specific Benchmarks

The success of domain-specific benchmarks in other fields provides both precedent and methodology for FinanceBench. In medicine, MedQA and related benchmarks revealed that models performing well on general knowledge tasks can fail dramatically on clinical reasoning, motivating the development of specialized evaluation suites. Similarly, LegalBench demonstrated that legal reasoning requires evaluation instruments distinct from general NLU benchmarks. These domain-specific efforts share a common finding: general benchmarks systematically overestimate model capability in specialized domains.

A key methodological challenge in domain-specific evaluation is establishing reliable ground truth. Artstein and Poesio (2008) provide the canonical treatment of inter-annotator agreement for computational linguistics, discussing when and how Cohen's $\kappa$ and Krippendorff's $\alpha$ should be applied to validate human annotations. The emerging paradigm of LLMs-as-Judges (2024) offers an alternative by using language models themselves as automated evaluators, though with documented biases including position bias and self-enhancement. FinanceBench sidesteps the subjectivity problem entirely through its deterministic computational oracle: ground truth is computed algorithmically via a validated 12-step calculation chain, eliminating annotation disagreement for all quantitative metrics. This oracle-based approach, to our knowledge unique among financial benchmarks, provides the methodological foundation for field-by-field accuracy measurement with well-defined error thresholds.

Following the successful paradigm of domain-specific benchmarks in medicine and law, FinanceBench provides the first comprehensive evaluation framework for structured financial analysis—uniquely leveraging a deterministic computational oracle to achieve reproducible, objective evaluation at scale.
