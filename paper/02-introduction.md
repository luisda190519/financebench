# 1. Introduction

Large language models are transforming how financial analysis is conducted across industries and geographies. Models such as GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA are being deployed for tasks ranging from sentiment analysis and risk assessment to cash flow projection and business viability evaluation (Li et al., 2023; Nie et al., 2024). This adoption is particularly consequential for small and medium enterprises in emerging markets, where access to professional financial advisors is limited and entrepreneurs increasingly rely on AI-generated projections to make investment decisions (Yang, 2025; Estefan et al., 2023). The Financial Stability Board (2024) has identified the rapid integration of AI into financial services as a systemic concern, noting that correlated errors across widely-used models could amplify risks rather than mitigate them. The democratization of financial analysis through AI is thus simultaneously an opportunity—extending analytical capabilities to previously underserved populations—and a risk, if the underlying models produce incorrect calculations that users cannot verify.

Yet despite this rapid adoption, a fundamental evaluation gap persists: no standardized benchmark measures whether LLMs can correctly perform structured financial calculations. Existing general-purpose benchmarks evaluate breadth but not financial depth. HELM (Liang et al., 2023) provides holistic multi-metric evaluation across 42 scenarios, yet finance is notably underrepresented among them. MMLU and MMLU-Pro (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2024) test knowledge across 57 domains including basic finance, but through multiple-choice questions that assess concept recognition rather than computational ability—a model can correctly define net present value without being able to compute one. BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2022) includes arithmetic tasks, but these are orders of magnitude simpler than multi-year financial projections. On the financial AI side, FinBen (Xie et al., 2024), the most comprehensive financial benchmark to date with 40+ datasets and 20+ tasks, focuses on NLP-oriented evaluation: information extraction, sentiment classification, summarization, and stock prediction. PIXIU (Xie et al., 2023) similarly centers on classification and prediction. FinQA (Chen et al., 2021) comes closest by requiring numerical reasoning over financial tables, but evaluates arithmetic over *existing* data rather than generation of *complete* financial projections from raw business inputs. The consequences of this gap are concrete: developers select models for financial applications without empirical evidence, entrepreneurs trust unverified AI-generated projections, and researchers cannot meaningfully compare findings across studies.

This gap is particularly concerning because financial viability analysis demands a type of reasoning that is known to challenge current LLMs. Unlike grade-school arithmetic (Cobbe et al., 2021) or competition mathematics (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), financial analysis is a *cascading computation*: revenue calculations flow through cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, EBITDA, depreciation, interest expense, taxes with loss carryforward, net profit, and finally into discounted cash flow metrics—a 12-step chain where each step depends on all previous results. A small error early in this chain—misapplying a compound growth rate or miscalculating frequency normalization—propagates through every subsequent computation, amplifying into large deviations in NPV, IRR, and payback period. NumericBench (Li et al., 2024) has shown that even GPT-4o fails systematically on seemingly simple numerical tasks; financial analysis compounds this difficulty with domain-specific conventions (French amortization, multi-scenario consistency, conditional tax rules) that general mathematical benchmarks do not capture (Wei et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025).

We introduce **FinanceBench**, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating large language models on structured financial analysis tasks. Our contributions are fivefold:

1. **A deterministic ground-truth oracle.** We implement a verified calculation engine that produces exact numerical results for all financial metrics—NPV via discounted cash flows with a custom fallback, IRR via Newton-Raphson iteration with bisection backup, French loan amortization, straight-line depreciation, and tax computation with loss carryforward. Unlike human-annotated benchmarks subject to inter-annotator disagreement (Artstein & Poesio, 2008), the oracle provides unambiguous ground truth for every field in every case.

2. **1,000 deterministic test cases.** Generated using a Mulberry32 seeded pseudo-random number generator across 10 business types representative of Latin American SMEs (cafeteria, software, retail, construction, manufacturing, consulting, e-commerce, agriculture, real estate, hospitality), three difficulty levels (simple, medium, complex), and four economic scenarios (optimistic, normal, pessimistic, controlled). Identical datasets can be regenerated from seeds, ensuring full reproducibility.

3. **A field-by-field comparison engine.** Each LLM response is compared against oracle values across up to 316 individual fields per case (4 scenarios × 79 metrics each), classified into three tiers: match (≤1% relative error), small difference (1–5%), and large difference (>5%). This granularity enables diagnosis of *where* in the computation chain errors originate, not merely *whether* the final answer is correct.

4. **Multi-model evaluation.** We systematically evaluate 10 state-of-the-art LLMs—including proprietary models (GPT-4o, GPT-4 Turbo, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 1.5 Pro) and open-source models (LLaMA 3 70B, Mistral Large, DeepSeek R1, Qwen 2.5 72B)—under standardized zero-shot conditions with identical prompts containing 17 explicit calculation rules.

5. **Open-source release.** The complete dataset, generation code, oracle implementation, comparison engine, prompt templates, and evaluation scripts are publicly available, following the open benchmark paradigm established by GLUE (Wang et al., 2019) and FinBen (Xie et al., 2024).

FinanceBench is designed to be *complementary* to existing financial benchmarks: where FinBen evaluates whether models can understand financial language, FinanceBench evaluates whether they can perform financial mathematics. Together, they provide a more complete picture of LLM capability in the financial domain.
